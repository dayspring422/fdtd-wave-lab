# Validation results

Generated by `node tests/validate.js`. Courant number S = 0.5, glass n = 1.5.

## 1. Phase velocity

A plane wave travels along x. Its phase velocity is measured from the field as v = ω/k, where k comes from the phase difference between neighbouring cells. "Yee theory" is the speed the discrete scheme should produce according to its dispersion relation; "error" is the difference from the exact continuum value (c in vacuum, c/n in glass).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | vacuum | 0.98000 | 0.97972 | 1.00000 | 2.000% |  |
| 12 | vacuum | 0.99209 | 0.99124 | 1.00000 | 0.791% | 2.29 |
| 16 | vacuum | 0.99622 | 0.99512 | 1.00000 | 0.378% | 2.57 |
| 24 | vacuum | 0.99914 | 0.99785 | 1.00000 | 0.086% | 3.65 |
| 8 | glass | 0.62773 | 0.62810 | 0.66667 | 5.840% |  |
| 12 | glass | 0.64991 | 0.65068 | 0.66667 | 2.513% | 2.08 |
| 16 | glass | 0.65828 | 0.65787 | 0.66667 | 1.258% | 2.40 |
| 24 | glass | 0.66347 | 0.66281 | 0.66667 | 0.479% | 2.38 |

The measured speed matches the Yee prediction closely at every resolution, which checks the implementation. The error against the exact speed falls roughly as 1/λ², the expected second-order convergence of the Yee scheme. Glass is harder to resolve because the wavelength inside it is shorter by a factor of n.

## 2. Refraction angle

A wide Gaussian beam (half-width 5λ) hits a vacuum/glass interface at about 25°. Everything is read from the field, in regions away from the source and clear of the reflected beam. Snell's law is conservation of the wavenumber along the interface, kᵧ, so the incident angle is taken from the measured kᵧ of the incoming beam (sin θ₁ = kᵧ/k₀). The transmitted angle θ₂ is the measured direction of the wave inside the glass. It is compared with Snell's law, and with the angle the Yee dispersion relation predicts for the same kᵧ. The geometry scales with the wavelength, so every run is the same physical experiment at a different resolution.

| cells per λ | θ₁ from measured kᵧ | θ₂ measured | θ₂ Snell | error vs Snell | θ₂ Yee theory | error vs Yee | order |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 25.245° | 15.705° | 16.519° | 4.929% | 15.705° | 0.006% |  |
| 12 | 25.328° | 16.224° | 16.571° | 2.093% | 16.231° | 0.046% | 2.11 |
| 16 | 25.363° | 16.398° | 16.593° | 1.173% | 16.405° | 0.046% | 2.01 |
| 24 | 25.394° | 16.515° | 16.612° | 0.585% | 16.530° | 0.092% | 1.71 |

The measured angle agrees with the Yee prediction to within 0.1% at every resolution, which checks the implementation. The difference from Snell's law comes from numerical dispersion: on a coarse grid the wave in glass travels slightly slower than c/n, so it bends a little more than Snell's law predicts. That difference falls roughly as 1/λ², the same second-order convergence as the wave speed. At the finest grid the measurement itself (finite beam, averaging region) starts to limit the observed order.

## 3. Absorbing boundary

A short pulse is launched from the centre of a 100 × 100 cell region surrounded by a 20-cell absorbing layer. The field is recorded at two probes 5 cells inside the region: one facing the nearest edge (the wave hits the boundary head-on) and one in the corner (the wave hits both edges at 45°). The same pulse is also run in a much larger domain whose edges are too far away to matter within the recording time. Any difference between the two runs at a probe is the wave reflected by the boundary.

| boundary | reflection, side probe | reflection, corner probe | energy left after 500 steps |
|---|---|---|---|
| graded loss (previous) | -39.9 dB (1.012%) | -34.0 dB (1.999%) | 4.84e-7 of peak |
| split-field PML | -103.4 dB (0.001%) | -99.4 dB (0.001%) | 9.84e-8 of peak |

Reflection is the largest difference between the test run and the reference run at that probe, relative to the peak of the incoming pulse. Both layers use the same loss profile (cubic grading, 20 cells, maximum normalized loss 0.35), so the comparison isolates the difference in method: the graded layer damps every field component equally, while the PML damps each component only along its own direction. The app uses the PML by default; the boundary switch in the interface lets you compare them live.
